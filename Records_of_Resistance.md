# Records of Resistance — Today's Stock (Project 3)

> AI 201 submission. Moments where I rejected or redirected AI output.
> "What AI gave me, why I rejected it, what I did instead."
> 6 entries total. (3+ required)

---

## Resistance 1 — Rejected KakaoTalk bot → ChatGPT Custom GPT

**What AI gave me:** An automated system that pushes stock information to mom through KakaoTalk

**Why I rejected it:** Automatic push notifications are information overload. Mom needs to pull information when she wants it — not have it pushed at her. That's the opposite of what we're solving. Also, a KakaoTalk bot has ongoing maintenance costs and no clear path to $0 permanence. Dad's ChatGPT Plus subscription is already paid for and already in mom's hands.

**What I did instead:** ChatGPT Custom GPT. Lives inside the app she already uses. She opens it when she wants it.

---

## Resistance 2 — Rejected allowing buy/sell recommendations → kept the absolute rule

**What AI gave me:** A framing that suggested providing "directional signals" within a "judgment support" context — implying it would be okay to point toward buying or selling under certain conditions

**Why I rejected it:** Under Korean financial law, unlicensed investment advice is illegal regardless of how it's framed. "Support" framing doesn't change the legal exposure. More importantly, mom would likely trust what the GPT says — which makes any harm real, not hypothetical.

**What I did instead:** The GPT never makes the decision. It lowers the cost of making the decision — explaining what signals mean, summarizing bullish and bearish cases, translating English, compressing the news. The boundary stays firm.

---

## Resistance 3 — Rejected Gemini/Claude platform suggestion → kept ChatGPT

**What AI gave me:** A feature comparison recommending Gemini or Claude API as potentially more flexible

**Why I rejected it:** Feature rankings don't determine platform. Mom is already using ChatGPT successfully for doctor visits. Zero new apps, zero new logins, zero new learning is the constraint — and that constraint comes from knowing my user, not from a spec sheet. The best tool is the one she already opens.

**What I did instead:** ChatGPT Custom GPT. Built on top of her existing habit and existing access (dad's subscription).

---

## Resistance 4 — Rejected automated morning briefing → kept pull-based structure

**What AI gave me:** A scheduled daily briefing that would automatically push a stock summary to mom every morning

**Why I rejected it:** One of mom's core pain points is "there's too much news." An automatic daily push recreates that problem. We're solving information overload, not adding to it.

**What I did instead:** Mom opens the GPT when she wants it. Four conversation starters lower the barrier to entry. She stays in control of when information comes to her.

---

## Resistance 5 — Rejected all-in-one GPT → topic-specific GPT family

**What AI gave me:** An "all-in-one Mom GPT" combining stocks, travel, health, and other domains in one place

**Why I rejected it:** Different tasks come with different vocabulary and different expectations. In a stock GPT, "how's it looking today?" means market conditions. In a travel GPT, it might mean weather. Mixing them creates confusion for a user whose mental model shifts by task. The thesis of this project is that tools should match tasks — and that starts with the structure of the GPT itself.

**What I did instead:** Topic-specific GPT family. Today's Stock is the first instance, built deeply for one task.

---

## Resistance 6 — Rejected "recommended stocks" conversation starter → "trending stocks" informational framing

**What AI gave me:** A conversation starter like "What stocks are worth paying attention to right now?" — recommendation-implied phrasing

**Why I rejected it:** "Worth paying attention to" reads as a recommendation. From mom's perspective, the GPT is telling her something is good. That directly conflicts with the absolute rule (no investment recommendations) and sets the wrong expectation from the first message she sees.

**What I did instead:** "What stocks are people talking about lately?" Same information — why a stock is in the news, what's bullish, what's bearish — but framed as information, not advice. The distinction matters both legally and as a design decision about what kind of tool this is.
